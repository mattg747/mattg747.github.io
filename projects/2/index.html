Overview

This project explores 2D filtering in the spatial and frequency domains. In Part 1 I implement convolution from scratch, visualize finite-difference derivatives, and compare them with derivative-of-Gaussian (DoG) filtering. In Part 2 I apply these ideas: unsharp masking for sharpening, hybrid images that change with viewing distance, and multiresolution (Laplacian/Gaussian stack) blending for seamless composites.

  • Languages / libs: Python, NumPy, SciPy, OpenCV, Matplotlib
  • Key topics: convolution, padding, finite differences, Gaussian blur, DoG, unsharp mask, Fourier analysis, Gaussian/Laplacian stacks, multi-resolution blending

Part 1.1 — Convolutions from Scratch

I implemented 2D convolution with four nested loops (reference), then a two-loop version using vectorized row/column ops, plus zero-padding. I validated results against scipy.signal.convolve2d and discussed boundary handling (zero, reflect) and runtime tradeoffs.

Part 1.2 — Finite Difference Operator

Derivatives computed with Dx = [[-1, 1]] and Dy = [[-1],[1]]. The gradient magnitude ‖∇I‖ = √(Ix2 + Iy2) is thresholded to produce binary edges (threshold chosen by visual tuning).

Part 1.3 — Derivative of Gaussian (DoG)

Smoothing with a Gaussian reduces noise before differentiation. I show results from (i) blur → finite differences and (ii) single DoG filters constructed by convolving a Gaussian with Dx, Dy. I also visualize the DoG kernels as images.

Part 2.1 — Image “Sharpening” (Unsharp Mask)

Unsharp masking adds scaled high frequencies back to the image: Isharp = I + α (I − G∗I). I also construct a single kernel K = (1+α)δ − αG to realize the same effect via one convolution.

Part 2.2 — Hybrid Images

I low-pass one image and high-pass another, then sum. Up close, high frequencies dominate; from afar, only low frequencies remain. I include Derek+Nutmeg and my own pair.

Part 2.3 — Gaussian & Laplacian Stacks

I build same-size stacks (no downsampling): repeated Gaussian blurs form the Gaussian stack; Laplacian levels are differences between successive Gaussian levels. These prep for multires blending.

What I Learned

  • Convolution details (padding, kernel flipping) matter; vectorization gives big speedups.
  • DoG reduces noise amplification compared to raw finite differences; orientations are fun to visualize in HSV.
  • Unsharp masking is just “add the highs” — clean to express as a single kernel.
  • Hybrid images make the frequency split (low vs high) perceptually obvious.
  • Laplacian/Gaussian stacks let you “hide” seams by blending per band; mask alignment is critical.
